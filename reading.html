Reading

Books that rearranged something. Notes, not reviews.

Biased toward old books and primary sources. Progress is slow and uneven — some of these I've studied, some I'm still inside.

Fiction and how to live

  • Franz Kafka — The Trial. Read it first as absurdism, then recognized the machinery: systems that judge you without telling you the charge. Every large institution I've touched since — universities, hospitals, hiring — has a chapter of this in it. The horror isn't cruelty. It's procedure.
  • Leo Tolstoy — The Death of Ivan Ilyich, How Much Land Does a Man Need?. Short enough to reread yearly, which I do. Tolstoy's question is always the same: what if the respectable life is the wasted one? Uncomfortable precisely because the respectable life is the one I'm best at performing.
  • Albert Camus — The Myth of Sisyphus. No built-in meaning; no excuse for surrender either. I find this bracing rather than bleak. Whether it coheres with wanting a value system, I'm still checking.

Cities, systems, institutions

  • Jane Jacobs — The Death and Life of Great American Cities. Look at the street, not the plan. Order emerges from use, not from zoning. Changed how I see Chennai as much as any European city — improvisation as urban intelligence, not failure.
  • James C. Scott — Seeing Like a State. Why institutions simplify what they govern, and what gets destroyed in the simplification. Pairs well with Kafka: one explains why the machine needs you legible, the other what it feels like from inside.
  • Carlota Perez — Technological Revolutions and Financial Capital. Technologies arrive with manias, then institutions rearrange themselves around the wreckage. Useful for AI: the model is early, the rearrangement is the main event.

Business and thinking

  • Clayton Christensen — The Innovator's Dilemma. Good management producing failure, because incentives and cost structures point the wrong way. Failure is usually structural, not personal. Reassuring and damning in equal measure.
  • Michael Porter — "What Is Strategy?" Strategy as choosing what not to do. Eleven pages, denser than most books. I reread it whenever I catch myself wanting everything.
  • Richard Hamming — "You and Your Research." Work on important problems, on purpose, with your best hours. The sting: most days I work on what's available instead. He noticed. He wrote it down. Now I can't unknow it.
  • Paul Graham's early essays. Dated in places. Still the clearest writing I know on noticing things others walk past and building things before asking permission.

France and societies

  • Fernand Braudel — The Mediterranean (selections). History at the speed of geography: climate, trade, daily bread. Kings pass through; the sea remains. This is how I want to understand France — not through headlines but through what persists.
  • Alexis de Tocqueville — Democracy in America (selections). A Frenchman explaining America to itself. The model for how to look at any society, including my own: an outsider's attention, held long enough to see what locals can't.
  • George Orwell's essays. "Politics and the English Language" most of all. If a sentence can't survive being written plainly, the thought probably can't either. I apply this to my own pages regularly, with mixed results.

Incomplete, and partly aspirational. I'll mark read-versus-reading more honestly over time.
