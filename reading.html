Reading

Working list. Notes are what I took from each, not summaries.

I aim to read a lot, with a bias toward old books, primary sources, and things that change how I see systems. If something here shaped your thinking differently, tell me.

Philosophy, values, and how to live

  • Albert Camus — The Myth of Sisyphus. The clearest statement of absurdism I've found. Life has no built-in meaning; that doesn't license apathy, it demands honesty. I'm still working out whether a coherent value system can sit comfortably alongside this. Probably yes — values as commitments I construct, not truths I discover.
  • Stoic selections — Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus. Useful as operating advice, less convincing as philosophy. What stuck: distinguish what I control, act on that fully, ignore the rest. What didn't: the temptation to use Stoicism to tolerate systems I should instead question.
  • Iris Murdoch — The Sovereignty of Good (essay). Attention as a moral act. How I look at things shapes who I become. I think about this when I notice my information diet shaping my values without my consent.

Technology and how change happens

  • Carlota Perez — Technological Revolutions and Financial Capital. The idea that technologies arrive with financial bubbles, institutional lag, and eventually new ways of organizing. Gave me a frame for AI: the model is early, the institutional rearrangement is the main event.
  • Clayton Christensen — The Innovator's Dilemma. Less about disruption as a buzzword, more about why good management produces failure when incentives and cost structures point the wrong way. Incentives explain more than incompetence.
  • James C. Scott — Seeing Like a State. Why large systems simplify things to control them — and what gets destroyed in that simplification. I return to this when thinking about organizations, AI deployment, and centralized planning.

Business, strategy, economics

  • Michael Porter — What Is Strategy? (HBR essay). Strategy as choosing what not to do, and as a system of reinforcing activities rather than a single move. Short and denser than most books on the subject.
  • Alfred Chandler — Scale and Scope (selections). How organizational capabilities, not just technology, determined who won industrial eras. Relevant to the current question: who captures value from AI — model builders, infrastructure owners, or distributors?
  • Daniel Kahneman — Thinking, Fast and Slow. Standard recommendation, but genuinely useful for noticing my own errors. What stuck less: the exotic biases. What stuck more: how unreliable intuition is without feedback.
  • Thomas Piketty — Capital in the Twenty-First Century (parts). Reading less for policy conclusions, more for the long view: how capital, growth, and institutions interact over centuries. Scale and time reveal structure.

Culture, history, societies

  • Fernand Braudel — The Mediterranean (selections). History at the scale of geography and everyday life, not just kings and wars. Shaped how I think about France and Europe: history as something still present in institutions, cities, and habits.
  • Alexis de Tocqueville — Democracy in America (selections). An outsider observing a society's operating system. A model for how I want to travel: inhabit a system, notice what locals can't see, ask what produced it.
  • George Orwell — essays, especially "Politics and the English Language." Clear writing as clear thinking. If a sentence can't survive being written plainly, the idea probably can't either.

Essays and papers I return to

  • Richard Hamming — "You and Your Research." On choosing important problems and deliberately allocating time to them. Uncomfortable because it exposes how easily I work on what's available rather than what's important.
  • Paul Graham — "How to Make Wealth," "Great Hackers" (early essays). Dated in places, but useful on building things, choosing problems, and why small groups with agency outperform large groups with process.
  • Benoit Mandelbrot / Geoffrey West-type work on scaling (various). How size changes behaviour in cities, companies, and organisms. I like ideas that work across domains — with the caveat that analogies can mislead.

This list is incomplete and partly aspirational — some of these I've studied closely, others I'm still working through. I'll mark that more precisely over time.
