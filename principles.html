Principles

Provisional. Last revised October 2026. These are hypotheses I'm testing, not conclusions.

The structure I keep returning to: fix identity first. Align philosophy and values. Then let systems, habits, and goals follow. Most self-improvement advice starts at the end, with tactics. I think that gets it backwards. If the identity is unclear, no workflow will hold.

I'm not sure whether values are discovered or constructed. Possibly both — discovered through experiment, then constructed through commitment. For now I act as if: try things, notice what feels non-negotiable, then design around it.

Identity and values

  • Identity precedes systems. Ask what kind of person I want to become, then ask what that person would do repeatedly. Habits follow identity more reliably than the reverse. The mistake may be treating discipline as a substitute for clarity.
  • Name the values explicitly. Mine, currently: freedom, responsibility, curiosity, depth, autonomy, connection, health. If a decision violates one of these, no amount of optimization elsewhere fixes it.
  • Distinguish mine from inherited. A lot of what I want may be status I absorbed rather than values I chose. The question underneath any ambition: is this actually what I value, or am I inheriting someone else's definition of success?
  • Allow tension. I want ambition and a peaceful life. I hold an absurdist view of life and want a coherent value system. I don't think this needs resolving yet. The tension itself is informative.

Thinking

  • Look for the system. Behaviour usually makes sense once you see the incentives, constraints, and values that produced it. If something looks irrational, I probably don't understand the system yet.
  • Find the hidden assumption. Most disagreements are not about conclusions but about unstated premises. Writing them down usually dissolves half the confusion.
  • Ask what happens at scale and over time. What looks harmless once may look different repeated a million times, or continued for twenty years. Scale and time reveal structure.
  • Check whether I'm solving the right problem. I have a tendency to optimize inside a given frame. The higher-leverage move is usually reframing the problem.
  • Prefer mechanisms over labels. In business especially: don't ask "is this a good company?" Ask what causes it to win — costs, distribution, switching costs, network effects, capital allocation, culture. Frameworks over opinions.
  • Write to think. If I can't write it plainly, I don't understand it. Short paragraphs, strong claim followed by qualification.

Learning

  • Learn fundamentals before tools. Tools change; fundamentals compound. In AI, in engineering, in business — understand the layer underneath before reaching for the abstraction.
  • Build things instead of only consuming information. Reading feels like progress but often isn't. A small working thing teaches more than ten tutorials.
  • Use projects as a forcing function. Pick a project slightly too hard, with a public or external deadline. Learning follows necessity.
  • Prefer first-principles understanding over memorisation. If I can re-derive it, I own it. If I can only recite it, I don't.
  • Connect ideas across domains. Depth in one field plus the ability to import models from others is rare and useful. I'm increasingly convinced range matters more than it appears to specialists.
  • Turn large goals into games. Levels, challenges, feedback loops, progression. I'm not sure why, but I learn faster when a goal feels like a game I'm designing rather than a duty I'm enduring. Currently testing this with French.
  • Depth over familiarity. It's easy to collect surface knowledge about many things. Harder and more valuable: genuine understanding of a few difficult subjects.

Work and career

  • Be suspicious of the default script. School → job → promotion → higher salary → retirement is one path, not the path. It optimizes for legibility, not necessarily for a good life.
  • Optimize for optionality and learning rate in my 20s. Skills, relationships, savings runway, and freedom to change direction. Prestige is a weak proxy for these.
  • Pick environments over titles. Who I work with, what I get to learn, and how much agency I have matter more than the brand name. I'm still testing how far to take this.
  • Protect the basics that buy freedom. Sleep, health, money buffer, time to think. These are not lifestyle preferences; they're what make risk-taking possible.
  • Say no by default. Every commitment has a hidden cost in attention. I want to say yes rarely and with intensity.
  • Move toward problems, not identities. Instead of "am I an engineer or a strategist?", ask "what problem is worth working on, and what skills does it require?" Identities can follow.

Technology and business

  • Ask what technology does to structure, not just tasks. The interesting question about AI is not which tasks get automated but how organizations, industries, and geopolitical power reconfigure around it.
  • Adoption matters more than capability. Working on biomedical AI taught me this. The model is often the easy part. Workflows, trust, regulation, incentives, and distribution decide what actually gets used.
  • Follow incentives and capital. Data centers, chips, talent flows, and balance sheets explain more about where technology goes than press releases do.
  • Study how industries evolve, not just how startups pitch. Cost structures, distribution, regulation, and timing explain most wins and most failures.

Life

  • Freedom requires structure. Unconstrained time drifts. A few non-negotiables — health, deep work, relationships — create more freedom than total flexibility does. A hypothesis I'm testing.
  • Calibrate ambition with enough. I want to build meaningful things and have time, travel, family, physical activity, hobbies. The question is not how to maximize one, but what level of each makes the whole work.
  • Take place seriously. Cities and cultures are systems that shape what feels normal — around work, status, food, relationships, aesthetics. Choosing where to live is choosing which system to inhabit. This is part of why France interests me.
  • Keep experiments small and reversible. A week-long version before a year-long version. Most decisions don't need courage; they need a cheaper test.
