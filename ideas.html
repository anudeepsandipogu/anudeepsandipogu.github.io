Technology changes structure, not just tasks

October 2026

When people discuss AI, they usually list tasks that will be automated. I think that's the shallow version.

The deeper change is structural: what happens to firms, industries, and careers when cognitive work gets cheap. Do organizations get smaller or larger? Does expertise centralize into infrastructure or fragment into agents? Does the advantage shift to distribution, trust, data, energy, or taste?

A hypothesis I'm testing: every general-purpose technology first looks like a better tool, then turns out to be a different organization chart. The question underneath AI adoption is not "what can the model do?" but "what does the firm look like after this?"

I'm not sure about the answer. But I notice I learn more from studying data centers, balance sheets, and hospital workflows than from model benchmarks.

Biomedical AI taught me about adoption, not algorithms

September 2026

Working on biomedical AI and medical imaging, the model was rarely the hardest part.

The hard parts were everything around it: messy data, unclear ground truth, clinicians with limited time, workflows that didn't want another dashboard, regulation, liability, and the basic question of who benefits enough to change behaviour.

I'm increasingly convinced this generalizes. Capability gets the demo; adoption gets the outcome. Incentives, trust, workflow fit, and distribution decide what survives.

The mistake may be treating deployment as an afterthought. In medicine at least, deployment is the problem.

Careers as systems to explore, not ladders to climb

September 2026

The default script — school → job → promotion → higher salary → retirement — optimizes for legibility. Everyone can tell whether you're ahead or behind.

I don't find that motivating. I'm more interested in careers that preserve optionality: skills that transfer, savings that buy time, relationships that open doors, and the ability to move between engineering, technology, strategy, and business as problems require.

This can look like indecision from the outside. The question I ask instead: what is the shape of work that fits me? That takes experiments to answer, not introspection alone.

One risk I see clearly: optimizing for optionality forever and never committing. The counter-hypothesis I'm holding: explore broadly in my 20s, then concentrate force once something earns it.

Identity first, systems second

August 2026

Most productivity advice starts with tactics: habits, workflows, tools. I've tried that. It doesn't hold if the identity underneath is unclear.

What seems to work better, at least for me: decide what kind of person I want to become, name the values explicitly, then design systems that a person like that would naturally follow.

Example: "I am someone who thinks clearly" leads to writing regularly. "I am someone who is physically capable" leads to training. The system follows the identity; willpower is a backup, not the plan.

I'm not sure whether values are discovered or constructed. For now I treat it as both: run experiments, notice what feels non-negotiable, then commit and build around it.

Turning goals into games

August 2026

I'm interested in deliberately constructing "games" for myself: levels, challenges, feedback loops, progression systems.

Large goals — learn French, understand economics, get genuinely good at something — feel abstract. Games make them concrete: what is level 1, what counts as points, where is the feedback, what unlocks next?

I'm currently testing this with French: daily streaks mean little; being able to hold a 5-minute conversation, read one article without translation, write one page — those are levels. I'm not sure gamification sustains effort long-term, but as a forcing function for starting, it works.

The question underneath: can I design environments where the behaviour I want is the easy and rewarding move, rather than relying on discipline?

What France represents to me (and why I might be wrong)

July 2026

My interest in France is not only about business school. It's about a different definition of a good life.

Different societies normalize different things: how much work is enough, what status means, what food, aesthetics, cities, and intellectual life are for, how individualism and social life balance. Travel, for me, is temporarily inhabiting a different system to see what it reveals about my own assumptions.

I might be romanticizing Europe. That's the risk. The test is not visiting but living somewhere long enough for the novelty to wear off — and noticing what still seems valuable after that.

The larger question: what kind of environment and culture do I want to build my adult life around? Credentials expire. Environments shape you daily.
