What people stop doing once it becomes cheap

October 2026

Most discussion of AI catalogues what the technology can do. That's the least interesting part. Capabilities are press releases; behaviour is the story.

The question I keep coming back to: what do people stop doing once cognition costs nothing? Drafting, memorizing, summarizing — the obvious ones. But also, possibly: asking carefully. When answers are free, the scarce thing becomes knowing what is worth asking.

This scales up awkwardly. A student, a firm, a hospital, a labour market — each stops doing something different, and none of them announces it. You only notice years later, the way you notice a city changed when a shop you never visited closes.

The model worked. Nothing else did.

September 2026

In my biomedical AI work, the model was rarely the problem. The problem was everything the diagram left out: the clinician with four minutes, the workflow that didn't want another dashboard, the question of who gets blamed when the machine is wrong.

There's a pattern here I've started calling "demo-complete": technically finished, practically homeless. It happens in medicine constantly, and I suspect it happens everywhere technology meets institutions.

The dry version: capability is necessary and almost never sufficient. The sufficient part — incentives, trust, habit — is unglamorous and therefore understudied. I find the unglamorous part more interesting now.

Careers people don't want

September 2026

Some of the sharpest people I know are optimising for careers they describe, in unguarded moments, with something like dread. Long hours toward a life they wouldn't choose if it weren't prestigious.

Kafka would have recognized the mechanism: a system that runs on everyone privately doubting it and publicly performing it. Nobody is forcing anyone. That's what makes it durable.

My own version of this: the pull toward impressive-sounding paths — strategy, elite schools, important companies. Each time I ask whether I want the work or the description of the work, the answer is embarrassing often enough that I keep asking.

France and the question of how much life belongs to work

August 2026

France has an unusually interesting answer to how much of life should belong to work. Lunch is not refuelling. August is not laziness. Aesthetics are not decoration. Whether it's a better answer or just one that appeals to my current fatigue, I can't yet tell.

What interests me isn't the policies — those are downstream. It's the conception of adulthood underneath: a serious person is not defined by output. Ambition exists but it shares the house with food, argument, beauty, leisure. The house has more rooms than the ones I grew up in.

The honest caveat: distance flatters. Every society looks wise from far away. The test is ordinary Tuesdays, in winter, with paperwork. I intend to take it before concluding anything.

Why some places make ordinary life feel expansive

August 2026

Some cities make a grocery run feel like living. Others make it feel like logistics. The difference isn't wealth — I've felt both in rich and poor places.

Jane Jacobs' old observation holds: it's the street, the mix, the density of unplanned encounter. A good city lowers the cost of serendipity. A bad one taxes it — through distance, through cars, through everything being single-use.

Chennai taught me the other half: improvisation as a system. When institutions are thin, people thicken. There's warmth in that, and also exhaustion. European order and Indian improvisation look like opposite solutions to different constraints. I think about which constraints I'd choose to live inside.

On caring about something else

July 2026

Maybe the opposite of burnout isn't working less. Maybe it's caring about something else.

Everyone I know who recovered from burnout describes the same sequence: not rest, exactly, but a second thing that mattered — a person, a place, a project with no career value. Rest restores energy. The second thing restores proportion.

This is suspiciously neat, and I distrust neatness in psychology. But as an observation about my own tired months, it keeps checking out. Exhaustion narrows the world to one room; the cure might be another room, not a dimmer switch.
